# Long-Form Article Template

## Specifications

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Word Count | 800-1,500 words |
| Subheadings | H2 every 200-300 words |
| Stories Required | Minimum 2 from story bank |
| Bold Phrases | 2-3 per section for skimmability |
| CTA | Single, clear, end of article |
| Tone | Authoritative but accessible. Written for the audience, not for the writer. |

---

## Article Structure

```
# [HEADLINE]

[OPENING: 2-3 paragraphs. Hook the reader with a story, bold claim, or question.
Set the frame for the entire article. The reader should know what they will
learn and why it matters within the first 150 words.]

## [SECTION 1 SUBHEAD]

[200-300 words. First major point. Anchored to a specific story or example.
Bold 2-3 key phrases. End with a transition to the next section.]

## [SECTION 2 SUBHEAD]

[200-300 words. Second major point. Builds on, contrasts with, or deepens
Section 1. Another story or specific example. Bold key phrases.]

## [SECTION 3 SUBHEAD]

[200-300 words. Third major point OR the "how-to" section. If the first
two sections established the "what" and "why", this is the "how."
Concrete, actionable, specific.]

## [CONCLUSION / TAKEAWAY SUBHEAD]

[100-150 words. Single clear lesson. Callback to the opening story.
CTA that flows naturally from the conclusion.]
```

## Headline Patterns

### Curiosity-Driven (Mueller, Brian Kurtz DNA)
- "The [Specific Thing] Nobody Talks About in [Industry]"
- "What [Specific Number] of [Audience] Taught Me About [Topic]"
- "I [Surprising Action] and [Unexpected Outcome]"
- "The [Time Period] That Changed How I Think About [Topic]"
- "Why [Common Practice] Is Costing You [Specific Outcome]"

### Benefit-Driven (Hormozi, Lead Gen Jay DNA)
- "How to [Achieve Specific Result] Without [Common Sacrifice]"
- "The [Number]-Step Process Behind [Specific Outcome]"
- "[Specific Result] in [Timeframe]: The Exact System"
- "How [Client/Company] Went From [Bad State] to [Good State]"

### Framework-Driven (Todd Brown, Jay Abraham DNA)
- "The [Named Framework] for [Desired Outcome]"
- "[Number] Types of [Problem] (And How to Fix Each One)"
- "The Hidden Mechanism Behind [Common Problem]"
- "Why [Solution A] Fails and [Solution B] Works"

### Identity-Driven (Tom Bilyeu DNA)
- "The Person You Need to Become to [Achieve Goal]"
- "Why [Mindset Shift] Matters More Than [Tactical Skill]"
- "The Real Reason [Audience] Stay Stuck at [Level]"

### Rules for Headlines
- No clickbait that the article does not deliver on
- Specific beats general ("127 Leads in 30 Days" beats "How to Get More Leads")
- Avoid starting with "How to" more than once per week
- The headline should pass the "would I click this in my feed?" test
- Under 80 characters for LinkedIn article titles
- Use numbers when you have them (odd numbers slightly outperform even numbers in testing)

## Opening Patterns

### Story Open (Mueller DNA)

Start with a story. Drop the reader into a scene. No throat-clearing.

```
[Scene-setting sentence. Time, place, or action.]

[Tension or complication. What went wrong, what was at stake.]

[The moment that made me realize this article needed to be written.]

[Transition to the article's thesis in 1-2 sentences.]
```

Example shape:
> Last March I sat across the table from a candidate who, on paper, was everything we wanted. Twenty minutes into the interview, my gut was screaming. I ignored it. That decision cost us $87K and two good employees. Here is what I should have been looking for instead, and the framework that has made every hire since then a good one.

### Bold Claim Open (Hormozi DNA)

Start with a provocative statement. Back it up immediately.

```
[Bold, contrarian, or surprising claim. One sentence.]

[Why this matters to the reader. Stakes.]

[Quick proof or credential -- why should the reader trust you on this.]

[Preview of what the article covers.]
```

Example shape:
> Everything you have been taught about hiring is optimized for the wrong outcome. You are screening for competence when you should be screening for character. I have hired over 200 people across four companies. The framework that finally worked has nothing to do with resumes.

### Question Open (Todd Brown DNA)

Start with a question that the reader cannot ignore because it describes their situation.

```
[Question that names the reader's exact problem.]

[Acknowledge their likely current approach -- show you understand.]

[Tease that there is a mechanism they are missing.]

[Preview the article's answer.]
```

Example shape:
> Why does your sales pipeline always stall at the same point? You have tried better scripts, more follow-ups, different offers. None of it moves the number past that invisible ceiling. The problem is not your effort. The problem is a hidden mechanism in your qualification process that is filtering out your best buyers.

### Insider Open (Brian Kurtz DNA)

Start with a credibility signal or insider knowledge that earns attention.

```
[Reference to earned experience, respected figure, or insider access.]

[What you learned that contradicts common knowledge.]

[Why you are sharing it now.]

[Preview.]
```

## Section Writing Rules

### Every Section Must Contain:

1. **A main point** stated clearly in the first 1-2 sentences of the section
2. **A story, example, or data point** that proves or illustrates the main point
3. **2-3 bold phrases** that allow a skimmer to get the gist without reading every word
4. **A transition** to the next section (can be a question, a contrast, or a simple bridge)

### Section Types

**The Story Section** (Mueller, Bilyeu DNA):
The main point is delivered through narrative. The section opens with action, builds tension, reveals the lesson. The bold phrases highlight the key moments.

**The Framework Section** (Todd Brown, Jay Abraham DNA):
The main point is a named concept or multi-step process. The section defines the framework, explains why it works, and gives one concrete example of it in action. Bold the framework name and key steps.

**The Tactical Section** (Lead Gen Jay, Liam Ottley DNA):
The main point is a specific how-to. The section walks through exact steps with enough detail that the reader could execute immediately. Bold the action items.

**The Contrarian Section** (Hormozi DNA):
The main point challenges a commonly held belief. The section states the common belief, dismantles it with logic or evidence, and offers the contrarian alternative. Bold the contrarian claim.

**The Authority Section** (Brian Kurtz DNA):
The main point is supported by insider experience or respected sources. The section establishes credibility, shares the insight, and connects it to the reader's world. Bold the insight.

## Conclusion Template

```
## [Concluding Subhead -- not "Conclusion" or "Final Thoughts"]

[Callback to the opening story or claim. 1-2 sentences that close the loop.]

[The single takeaway. One sentence that captures the entire article. Bold this.]

[Bridge to CTA. How the takeaway connects to what the reader should do next.]

[CTA. Single, specific, low-friction.]
```

**Concluding Subhead Examples** (not "Conclusion"):
- "The One Thing That Changed Everything"
- "What Happened Next"
- "The Hire That Proved the Framework"
- "Where This Leaves You"
- "The Math That Settles the Debate"

## Formatting Rules

- **Paragraphs**: 2-4 sentences each. No walls of text.
- **Bold**: Use bold for key phrases that a skimmer should catch. Do not bold entire sentences. Bold the 3-5 most important words or short phrases.
- **Lists**: Use sparingly. If you need a numbered list, keep it to 3-5 items and make each item substantive (not filler). Bullet lists for process steps or examples.
- **Block quotes**: Use for direct quotes from the story bank or from the client's interview transcripts. Max one block quote per article.
- **Links**: Max 2 external links per article. One in the body (to relevant resource), one in the CTA.
- **Images**: Reference where images should go with `[IMAGE: description]` tags but do not generate images. The content manager will source them.

---

## Article-Specific Anti-Slop Rules

In addition to the global anti-slop rules:

- Do not use "In this article, we'll explore" or any variant. Just start.
- Do not end sections with "Let's move on to" or "Now let's look at." Just start the next section.
- Do not begin the conclusion with "In conclusion" or "To sum up" or "In summary." Just deliver the takeaway.
- Do not use "As we've seen" or "As mentioned earlier." If you need to reference a prior section, do it with a specific detail, not a meta-reference.
- Subheadings should be interesting, not functional. "The $87K Mistake" beats "Section 1: Hiring Errors." "Why Gut Instinct Is Data" beats "The Importance of Intuition."
