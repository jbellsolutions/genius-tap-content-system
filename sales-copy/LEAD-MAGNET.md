## PAGE 2: THE "CHAPTERS" EXERCISE

---

### Your Career Is a Book. Name the Chapters.

This is the single most powerful exercise we've found for unlocking an expert's content. It takes about five minutes and it will change how you think about your own story.

Here's how it works:

**Imagine your career is a book. Not a resume — a BOOK. With chapters that have titles, turning points, and characters.**

Grab a piece of paper (or open your notes app) and name your chapters. Most people have 4-7. Don't overthink it. Go with your gut.

Here are some prompts to help:

**Chapter-Finding Questions:**

1. What was the moment you chose this career path — or did it choose you? What were you doing the day before everything changed direction?

2. Who was the mentor, boss, or rival who fundamentally altered how you see your work? What did they say or do that rewired your thinking?

3. What was the biggest professional failure or near-failure you've experienced? What happened in the 48 hours after it went wrong?

4. When did you first realize you were genuinely good at what you do? What was the specific moment — not the gradual realization, the MOMENT?

5. What's the most important lesson you learned that you wish you could go back and teach your younger self? What's the story that taught you that lesson?

6. Is there a period of your career you almost never talk about? Why?

7. What would the final chapter be titled — the chapter you're living right now?

**Example: How One Client Named His Chapters**

Tony D'Agostino, a Navy veteran turned consultant, named his chapters like this:

- Chapter 1: "Small Town Kid With Something to Prove"
- Chapter 2: "The Navy Made Me Someone I Didn't Recognize"
- Chapter 3: "The Deployment That Changed Everything"
- Chapter 4: "Coming Home to a World That Moved On"
- Chapter 5: "Building Something That Matters"

From those five chapter titles alone, we extracted 17 stories in a single conversation. Each chapter contained 3-4 moments he had never framed as "content" before.

**Your turn.** Write your chapter titles now. Don't read ahead until you do.

Seriously. Do it now.

Those chapter titles? Each one contains a minimum of 3 stories. Each story can become 3-4 pieces of content. If you named 5 chapters, you're looking at 45-60 pieces of content that are uniquely, unmistakably yours.

You just built the foundation of a content strategy in five minutes.

## PAGE 3: BEFORE/AFTER EXAMPLES

---

### The Transformation: Generic vs. Story-Driven Content

The difference between content that gets scrolled past and content that gets saved, shared, and remembered almost always comes down to one thing: specificity.

Here's what we mean.

---

**BEFORE: Generic Expert Post**

*"Great leaders know how to listen. In my experience, the best managers ask more questions than they answer. Active listening is the most underrated leadership skill. If you want to build trust with your team, start by really hearing what they have to say. #leadership #management #listening"*

This post is... fine. It's also indistinguishable from 10,000 other posts on LinkedIn right now. There's no story. No specificity. No reason to believe this person has actually LIVED this lesson.

---

**AFTER: Story-Driven Expert Post**

*"In 2014, I almost fired my best engineer.*

*She'd gone quiet in meetings. Missed two deadlines. Her code reviews were getting sloppy. I was drafting the PIP when my mentor, Carol, stopped me in the hallway.*

*'Have you asked her what's wrong?'*

*'She knows she can come to me.'*

*'That's not what I asked.'*

*I sat down with Sarah the next day. No agenda. No feedback form. Just: 'Hey — what's going on? And I mean really.'*

*Forty-five minutes later, I learned she was going through a divorce, her mother had been diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's, and she'd been sleeping in her car twice a week because she couldn't face going home to an empty apartment.*

*I didn't fire Sarah. I gave her flex time and connected her with our EAP. She went on to architect the system that generated $4M in new revenue that year.*

*Carol taught me something that day that I've carried for a decade: 'Have you asked?' is the most powerful management question in existence.*

*Not 'have you told them the expectations?' Not 'have you documented the performance issues?'*

*Have. You. Asked."*

---

**What changed?**

The lesson is identical: listen to your people. But the second version is UNFORGETTABLE because:

- It names real people (Sarah, Carol)
- It includes a specific detail only the author would know (sleeping in the car twice a week)
- It has a turning point with dialogue you can hear
- It leads to a concrete outcome ($4M in revenue)
- Nobody else could have written it

That's the difference between thought leadership and thought decoration.

Every expert has dozens of stories like this. They just need to be extracted.

## PAGE 4: THE 5 TYPES OF STORIES EVERY EXPERT HAS (AND DOESN'T KNOW IT)

---

### You Have More Stories Than You Think

After hundreds of extraction conversations, we've identified five story types that every expert carries — regardless of industry, experience level, or personality. Most experts don't recognize these as "content" because they feel too personal, too specific, or too small.

They're not. They're exactly what your audience is starving for.

---

**STORY TYPE 1: The Origin Story**

*The moment you chose this path — or it chose you.*

This isn't "I've always been passionate about finance." This is the SPECIFIC moment. The conversation. The event. The accident, the frustration, the epiphany that made you think: "This is what I'm supposed to do."

Everyone has one. Most people have never articulated it clearly.

**Prompt to find yours:** Close your eyes. What were you doing the day before you decided to pursue your current career? What happened the next day that changed everything?

---

**STORY TYPE 2: The Mentor Story**

*The person who saw something in you before you saw it yourself.*

Not a generic "I had great mentors" story. A SPECIFIC person. A specific thing they said. A specific moment where their words or actions altered your trajectory.

The best mentor stories include the moment of friction — the time the mentor told you something you didn't want to hear, and you only understood why years later.

**Prompt to find yours:** Who said the single most important sentence of your professional life? What were the exact words? Where were you standing when they said it?

---

**STORY TYPE 3: The Failure-to-Breakthrough Story**

*The time everything fell apart and what emerged from the rubble.*

This is the story experts are most afraid to tell and the one audiences respond to most powerfully. Not because people enjoy watching others fail — but because the SPECIFIC way you recovered reveals more about your expertise than any success story ever could.

**Prompt to find yours:** What's the professional moment you're most embarrassed about? What did you learn in the 30 days that followed that you couldn't have learned any other way?

---

**STORY TYPE 4: The "I Was There" Story**

*The industry moment, the historical event, the shift you witnessed firsthand.*

You were in the room when the decision was made. You saw the market shift before anyone else named it. You were present for the moment that everyone in your industry talks about — but you have the version nobody else has.

These stories carry enormous authority because they place you at the intersection of personal experience and historical significance.

**Prompt to find yours:** What's the biggest shift your industry has undergone in your career? Where were you when it happened? What did you see that the official narrative leaves out?

---

**STORY TYPE 5: The Paying-It-Forward Story**

*The time you helped someone the way you wished you'd been helped.*

This is the story that reveals your values without you having to state them. The mentee you championed. The colleague you supported through a crisis. The decision you made that cost you something but helped someone else.

These stories are magnetic because they show CHARACTER, not just competence.

**Prompt to find yours:** Who is the person you're most proud of helping in your career? What did you do for them? What did it cost you? Would you do it again?

---

You now have five story types and five prompts. If you spend 10 minutes per prompt, you'll have the raw material for 3-6 months of content by the end of the hour.

## PAGE 6: THE ANTI-AI-SLOP CHECKLIST

---

### 7 Questions to Ask Before Publishing Any Content

AI-generated content is flooding every platform. Most of it is technically correct, grammatically clean, and completely forgettable. It reads like it was written by a very smart person who has never actually done the thing they're writing about.

Your content cannot sound like that. Not because AI is bad — but because YOUR stories deserve better than the same treatment every other prompt gets.

Before you publish anything with your name on it, run it through these seven questions:

---

**1. Does it name a real person?**

Not "a client" or "someone I know" — a real name. (With permission, obviously.) Names create trust because they signal: "This actually happened. I'm not making this up." If your content is full of unnamed characters, it reads like a parable, not an experience.

**2. Does it include a specific detail only someone who was there would know?**

The temperature in the room. The color of the whiteboard marker. The thing someone was eating when they said the important thing. These micro-details are impossible to fake and immediately signal authenticity.

**3. Could someone else have written this?**

This is the most important question on the list. If you swap out your name and photo, could this post belong to any other expert in your field? If yes, it's not thought leadership. It's thought decoration. Rewrite it until the answer is no.

**4. Does it contain a direct quote from memory?**

Not a polished, perfect quote. A real one. The kind that's slightly awkward, uses the wrong grammar, or includes a word you wouldn't normally use. Quoted speech from real conversations is the fastest way to prove your content comes from lived experience.

**5. Is there a moment of tension or surprise?**

Every piece of content worth reading has a turn. A moment where the expected thing doesn't happen. Where the lesson gets complicated. Where you were wrong before you were right. If your content moves in a straight line from problem to solution, it's missing the part that makes people feel something.

**6. Would you tell this story at dinner?**

Read your content out loud. Does it sound like something you'd actually say to a friend over a meal? Or does it sound like a textbook with line breaks? If you wouldn't say it out loud, don't publish it. Your written voice should match your spoken voice — just more structured.

**7. Does it risk making you look imperfect?**

The most powerful content includes vulnerability. Not performative vulnerability — real uncertainty, real mistakes, real moments where you didn't have the answer. If every piece of content you publish makes you look brilliant, your audience will stop trusting you. Nobody is brilliant all the time, and everyone knows it.

---

**Score yourself:** If your content passes 5 out of 7, publish it. If it passes fewer than 4, it needs more of YOU in it. Go back to your stories.
